Seven-step technical intake

Describe the invention clearly enough to review, test and refine.

Organize the technical problem, changed features, mechanism, evidence, implementation details, alternatives, drawings and disclosure history into a structured draft.

Important: This tool does not determine inventorship, ownership, patentability, validity, infringement, freedom to operate or filing deadlines. The draft requires inventor and qualified patent-professional review. Do not enter confidential information on an unapproved device or browser.
Before you begin

Prepare technical facts, not patent language.

Answer in your own engineering words. Unknown information should remain visible rather than being invented.

Useful source material
  • design and architecture notes;
  • laboratory/test records and baselines;
  • drawings, flow diagrams and interface definitions;
  • known products, papers, standards or patents;
  • dates/audiences for any past or planned disclosure.
What the guide does Structures supplied information and identifies gaps.
What the guide does not do It does not call an AI model, search databases, draft claims or give legal advice.
Estimated time depends on technical complexity.
Step 1

Invention and project context

Identify the technical subject, contributors and matter context without deciding inventorship or ownership.

Writing prompt Use a working technical title. Avoid promotional terms such as “revolutionary,” “smart” or “best” unless they describe a defined technical feature.
Example form: “Method and system for [technical function] using [distinguishing technical feature].”
Include adjacent fields when interfaces matter.
List who contributed to which technical concepts; counsel determines inventorship.
For ownership review only; the form does not determine ownership.
Step 2

Background, baseline and known approaches

Explain how the problem is addressed today and under which conditions the known approaches fall short.

Legal-language caution Describe known references factually. Do not admit that a document is legally prior art or that a feature is conventional; leave those conclusions to counsel.
Example of a useful limitation statement
Under [defined load/temperature/dataset], the baseline exhibits [measured response ± uncertainty], which exceeds/falls below [requirement] because [supported mechanism or open hypothesis].
Step 3

Technical problem, core change and effect

Connect the proposed technical change to an effect through a mechanism and distinguish measured results from expectations.

Structure Given [baseline/problem], change [feature or relationship], operating through [mechanism], to produce [technical effect under conditions].
Step 4

Architecture, sequence, parameters and alternatives

Describe the complete technical solution and how its parts interact.

Completeness check Identify inputs, transformations, states, outputs, interfaces, ordering, units, conditions, required/optional elements and failure handling.
Step 5

Embodiments, experiments and drawings

Provide enough detail for a technical reader to understand how to make, run or test the proposal at its current stage.

Evidence currently available
Step 6

Priorities, workarounds and disclosure timing

Record what matters, how others might implement alternatives, and any public/private disclosures for professional review.

Do not calculate legal consequences here. Record exact facts, dates, audiences, confidentiality and documents. Patent professionals will assess jurisdiction-specific implications.
Step 7

Structured disclosure draft and gap review

Review every statement for technical accuracy. “Needs input” remains visible where the source material is incomplete.

Required review

  • Inventors confirm technical accuracy and contributor facts.
  • Technical reviewers confirm mechanism, parameters, evidence and drawings.
  • Qualified patent professionals assess inventorship, ownership, disclosure timing, patentability and filing requirements.
Technical completeness review
Problem and baseline
  • Is the response or technical problem defined?
  • Are the baseline, conditions and comparison method explicit?
  • Are units, sample size and uncertainty included where relevant?
  • Are legal characterizations of known references avoided?
Core change and mechanism
  • Does the draft identify a changed feature or relationship?
  • Does a technical mechanism connect the change to the effect?
  • Are required and optional features distinguished?
  • Are assumptions and boundary conditions visible?
Architecture and process
  • Are components, interfaces, states, inputs and outputs reconciled?
  • Are step order, branching, feedback and termination described?
  • Do parameter values include units and selection rationale?
  • Are dependencies and failure handling documented?
Alternatives and scope support
  • Are technically credible substitutions and variants described?
  • Are likely workarounds identified without legal conclusions?
  • Does each alternative retain a mechanism and expected effect?
  • Are infeasible or failed alternatives explained?
Evidence and reproducibility review
Implementation
  • Can a technical reader identify materials, equipment, data or components?
  • Are setup, initial state and operating conditions described?
  • Are inputs, transformations and outputs traceable?
  • Are implementation gaps labeled rather than silently filled?
Experiments and results
  • Are controls, replicates and comparison baselines stated?
  • Are results separated from predictions?
  • Are raw-data or notebook locations referenced under approved access?
  • Are negative and contradictory results retained?
Software and AI-specific support
  • Are data origin, preprocessing, splits and leakage controls described?
  • Are model architecture, training objective and inference flow described?
  • Are evaluation metrics, comparator and deployment constraints stated?
  • Are human, safety and failure-monitoring controls included where relevant?
Materials and device-specific support
  • Are composition, structure, processing and measurement methods explicit?
  • Are ranges supported by examples or physical rationale?
  • Are environmental, aging and manufacturing conditions stated?
  • Are analytical methods and detection limits documented?
Drawing and terminology review
Figures
  • Every figure has a number, title and purpose.
  • Every label is defined consistently in the text.
  • Arrows and flows have an explained direction.
  • Alternatives are not accidentally presented as mandatory.
Terminology
  • Acronyms are defined at first use.
  • The same component or step uses one consistent name.
  • Relative terms such as high, fast or thin have a reference.
  • Translated terms preserve the original technical meaning.
Disclosure-event and professional-review record
Disclosure facts
  • Exact date and time where material.
  • Audience, location/platform and materials disclosed.
  • Confidentiality agreement or access restriction facts.
  • Copies, URLs, invitations or presentation files retained.
Questions for counsel
  • Who contributed to each claimed technical concept?
  • Which agreements may affect ownership or obligations?
  • Which jurisdictions and deadlines require assessment?
  • What search, drafting and figure work remains?
Safe handling and export review
Before copy or download
  • Confirm the device, browser and matter are approved.
  • Close unapproved screen-sharing or recording tools.
  • Consider clipboard history and browser extensions.
  • Choose an approved matter-storage destination.
After export
  • Move the draft from Downloads to the approved matter location.
  • Apply access, retention and deletion controls.
  • Clear this page and sensitive clipboard content when required.
  • Do not circulate an unreviewed draft as a patent application.
